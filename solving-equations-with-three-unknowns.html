Solving Equations with Three Unknowns

Jason Moore

This is a guide on solving equations with three unknowns.

When working with a system of three linear equations involving three variables, the objective is to determine the values of those variables that satisfy all equations simultaneously. The method presented here follows a systematic process of successive elimination, in which one variable is removed at a time until a single equation in one unknown remains. Once that value is found, it can be substituted back into the earlier equations to determine the remaining unknowns. This approach is both reliable and efficient, provided that the order of elimination is chosen thoughtfully to simplify the arithmetic.

Consider the following system of equations:

3x+2y+4z=13x+2y+4z=1−x+y+2z=2−x+y+2z=2x−3y+z=−1x−3y+z=−1

The goal is to find the values of xx, yy, and zz that satisfy all three equations at once. To begin, it is helpful to examine the equations and decide which variable can be eliminated most conveniently. In this case, adding the second and third equations eliminates xx immediately, because the coefficients of xx in those two equations are opposites of one another. Carrying out this addition yields:

y−3y+3z=2−1y−3y+3z=2−1

which simplifies to:

−2y+3z=1−2y+3z=1

This newly obtained equation involves only yy and zz, reducing the complexity of the problem.

Next, the goal is to eliminate xx from another pair of equations so that a second equation in only yy and zz can be formed. To accomplish this, the second equation is multiplied by 3 and then added to the first equation. This operation produces:

2y+3y+4z+6z=1+62y+3y+4z+6z=1+6

which simplifies to:

5y+10z=75y+10z=7

At this stage, two equations involving only yy and zz have been obtained. These two equations form a smaller system that can be solved using the same elimination strategy applied previously. The two equations are:

−2y+3z=1−2y+3z=15y+10z=75y+10z=7

15z+20z=5+1415z+20z=5+14

To summarize, the process of solving a system of three linear equations in three unknowns involves selecting an efficient order of elimination, systematically removing one variable at a time, solving the resulting single-variable equation, and then back-substituting to find the remaining variables. This method is general and can be applied to a wide variety of linear systems, though the arithmetic may vary depending on the coefficients involved. With practice, the selection of the most convenient elimination order becomes increasingly intuitive, allowing for both accuracy and efficiency in obtaining the final solution.
